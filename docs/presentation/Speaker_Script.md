## The message to return to

> Remington helped pave the way for generations of American manufacturing. Our responsibility is to continue that legacy: rethink how engineering projects begin, reach the floor, and become useful to the people running production. We want Remington and The Kinetic Group to write the next rules of manufacturing engineering—and build the capabilities the next generation will inherit.

**The project thread:** Start with a real question from the operation. Build with the people who will rely on the answer. Test under real conditions. Put the result into a usable workflow. Keep improving it after deployment. Use this thread to explain *how* each project was approached, not just what technology it uses. “Writing the rules” is the ambition; this repeatable way of working is how we pursue it.

**Company map:** The Kinetic Group is the broader ammunition organization; Remington is one of its five brands. Brad's teams work across TKG and mainly at Remington in Lonoke. The three floor examples are live and still being polished. Public sentiment and Remi show the wider range of engineering work; do not describe them as production-line deployments.

## 4. Engineering that reaches the floor — Brad

“Sense, decide, act. We capture a machine signal or camera image. Software turns it into something useful. A person or equipment responds through the right workflow.”

“The three words are simple. Here is the way we try to carry a project through: begin with a real question, build with the people who need the answer, test in the conditions where it will run, and keep refining it after it reaches the floor. Was the signal trustworthy? Did the result arrive in time? Could someone understand and use it? We do not measure completion by whether a demo worked once. We measure whether the capability holds up in the workflow.”

## 6. SCADA — Brad

“SCADA gives us shared visibility into machine and process signals. Think of two questions: what is happening now, and what changed? A signal alone can be hard to interpret. With time and context in a production history, operators and engineers can investigate from the same record.”

“This project begins with the floor question, not the screen. Which signals matter? What context helps someone act? A display can look impressive but still fail to answer the question that sent an engineer looking. SCADA is live, and we are polishing it with the people who use it. That is one rule we are writing into our work: build visibility around decisions, not around data for its own sake.”

## 12. Live demonstration — Brad + Wesley, 20 minutes

**Opening (1 minute). Brad:** “We will demonstrate the same sense–decide–act pattern with something familiar: Gatorade bottles. This setup is for this event and is separate from our production inspection systems. As you watch, think like our engineering team: what question are we trying to answer, what evidence do we have, and what would make the result useful to someone running a line?”

**Round 1 (4 minutes):** Brad defines the task in one sentence, then places a bottle. Wesley narrates the camera view and on-screen classification or sorting decision. Brad asks what the system had to notice. Repeat once so the pattern is clear. **Point:** A project starts with a decision someone needs to make.

**Round 2 (5 minutes):** Wesley changes one visible condition or selects another bottle or feature. Brad asks the audience to predict the result before running it. Wesley explains what changed. If surprised, say: “That gives us something useful to test.” **Point:** Testing variation exposes what a successful first run cannot tell us.

**Engineering questions (5 minutes):** Ask: “If this had to work all day on a real line, what would you test next?” Take answers. Wesley discusses variation, timing, observability, and user feedback at a public-safe level. Brad connects the answers to the people who would depend on the result. **Point:** A result becomes an engineering capability when it can be used, checked, and improved in real conditions.

**Optional feature (3 minutes):** Show one additional feature only if it adds a new lesson. Keep cause and result visible; avoid a feature tour.

**Transition (2 minutes). Brad:** “You have just seen the beginning of how we approach a project: define the real decision, make a system that can respond, change the conditions, and learn from the result. In production, we take it further. We work with the people on the line until that capability is reliable and useful in their workflow. That is what we mean by writing new rules for how engineering work gets done.”

**If the demo stalls:** Show the camera view and last successful result. Ask the room what they would inspect first. Move on after two minutes instead of troubleshooting for the whole session.
